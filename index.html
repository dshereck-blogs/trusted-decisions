Decision engineering · The short version

In brief: what AI needs before you let it act

One major thunderstorm, one airline, and more code than a business reader should have to see. This page is the five-minute summary of the nine posts that follow, written for business leaders. If you want the technical underpinnings, go straight to the posts.

The short version

The core argument

Getting an AI agent to make a good decision is hard. Getting hundreds of them to act without causing an incident you have to explain to the board is harder. Right now, very few enterprises can prove who authorized an agent's actions, why the agent took that action, and what it broke next. The whole series comes down to one sentence: every consequential business decision should have one verifiable record that connects its context, its authority, its execution and its outcome, whether a person, a rule, a model or an AI agent made it.

Decision engineering is the discipline that produces that record.

Throughout this series, I use one fictional scenario to make the ideas concrete: a thunderstorm at an airline's main hub that strands thousands of passengers.

Why this is now your problem

Automated decisions are not new; credit scoring has run on them for decades. What is new is how much AI now does on its own. A wrong answer in a chat window was corrected by whoever read it, usually with a sigh. A wrong action that rebooks passengers or releases payments takes effect before anyone reviews it, so the controls have to work before the action, not after. Somewhere in your organization AI already acts. The question is how to let it make bigger decisions without learning about its mistakes from a customer, an auditor or the finance team.

Two costs you are already paying

Decision latency is the time from something happening in the business to the action being confirmed: the passenger rebooked, the payment released. Every team measures its own slice, and nobody owns it end to end.

Governance latency is the time between a rule being broken and the organization finding out. Most enterprises already have the logs, the policies and the approval records. They just live in six different systems that only meet during an audit, usually via a frantically assembled spreadsheet.

Shorter latencies show up in the numbers a CFO already tracks: issues resolved sooner, less spent on each decision, fewer avoidable losses. The goal is to eliminate the wait, not the judgment.

Will you really have hundreds of agents?

A word on agents. An AI agent is software that can act on its own rather than only answer questions. In this series, agent always means an AI agent, and people are called by their roles.

Back to that storm. Does any single process need hundreds of agents? No. Rebooking one passenger needs one agent, maybe a few. So when do you need hundreds? In two situations:

  • Breadth: an agent or two for every employee (and eventually more), which means your employees' agents will soon be ignoring each other's emails on their behalf.
  • Depth: one part of the business, such as disruption recovery or claims, with hundreds of agents working at once, rebooking passengers into the same limited seats.

Depth is where the consequential cases are today, so that is where this series starts.

One storm, one airline, 470 AI agents

A thunderstorm brings a 90-minute ground stop at an airline's main hub, disrupting 300 flights and 48,500 passengers. At the peak, 470 AI agents are running, doing fourteen different jobs. That doesn't mean 470 agents all talking at once. It works like the airport on a bad day: small teams, one for each open case (a disrupted flight, a stranded group, a bag that must follow its passenger), all reading the same departures board and competing for the same seats, crews and hotel rooms.

In this scenario, a group of fourteen passengers misses its connection, and two agents reach for the last seats at the same time. The reservation system tells one of them "no", which is annoying for the agent but a triumph for the architecture. When the ground stop lifts, agents build a recovery plan for forty stranded flights, but nothing is final until a duty manager approves it. Nobody watched all 470 agents. The controls worked anyway, because at that scale, nobody is watching each decision.

Most decisions should be handled by rules or models, and only the hard ones should reach an AI agent.

Four questions before you delegate a decision

  1. Is it based on facts that are current enough? Not last night's report. Not what the agent happened to fetch a minute before another agent changed it.
  2. Is it made by the right decider? A rule, a model, an agent or a person, chosen on cost, risk and speed rather than on whichever one looked best in a demo.
  3. Can the AI act only within limits you set? Limits enforced by software at the moment of the action, not by a line in the prompt that says please.
  4. Can you show who decided, why, under what authority and with what result? Months later, to someone who was not in the room and is not inclined to take your word for it.

If the honest answer to any of these is "we think so," you have found your first project. Answering them with confidence is what I call decision engineering: routing current facts to the right decider, checking every action against its limits before it happens, and recording what was decided and what followed.

A decider is whatever makes the call: a rule, a predictive model, an AI agent or a person.

What makes a decision trustworthy

You can trust a decision when you can answer six questions about it:

  1. Who made it?
  2. Did the decider declare its intent before acting?
  3. Can you replay it from the logs later and, given the same inputs, get the same answer?
  4. Did it stay inside its guardrails?
  5. If it goes sideways, can you undo it (or at least clean up the mess)?
  6. Was it a good decision? Number six is the one everyone forgets. A decision can be sub-second, 100% policy-compliant, immutably audited, and still completely wrong.

What this is not

Decision engineering is not for every decision: one low-risk decision living happily inside a well-run application should be left alone. It is not a product you buy off the shelf. Confluent, now part of IBM, provides the real-time foundation, and I am not going to pretend to be neutral about that, but the hard work is connecting what you already own.

How far to let AI go

Borrowing from the car industry's 0 to 5 scale for self-driving cars, here is a way to set how far AI can go. The car industry learned that the risky moment is the handover between machine and person. The handover is exactly where the controls should sit. In the storm, holding a flight for connecting passengers runs at level 4, rebooking a missed connection at level 3, and canceling a flight stays at level 2: the agents propose, a person decides.

LevelWhat happens
0 · ManualPeople decide and act.
1 · AssistedAI supplies context and predictions; people decide.
2 · RecommendAI proposes; a person approves each decision.
3 · SupervisedAI acts on routine cases within limits; exceptions go to people.
4 · BoundedAI acts across the process within enforced limits; people set policy and handle escalations.
5 · FullAI acts in all conditions with no person involved.

Most operations centers run at level 1 today. For consequential decisions, level 5 may not be the goal, and a decision moves up a level only on evidence.

What you can realistically achieve in 90 days

Start small. Pick one decision, make it trusted from end to end, and measure what changes. Ninety days is realistic for that, assuming the data, the system access and an actual business owner exist. If they don't, congratulations: you've found your first gap (missing data, access or an owner) in week one instead of week ten.

  1. Record a baselineMeasure how the decision is made today, so you can compare.
  2. Test alongside the old wayTest the new way of deciding alongside the old one, with only the old one acting.
  3. Act within tight limitsOnce it proves itself, let it act within tight limits.
  4. Pass the control testsBefore it gets more authority, it has to pass control tests that break things on purpose: stale data, two agents booking the same seat, a system failing halfway through a transaction.

That gives you real evidence before you scale it. To get the conversation started with your business, all you need is four things: the decision, its owner, the systems it touches and the measure the business will judge it by. If your four things look different from mine, I would like to hear why: find me on LinkedIn.

Decision engineering · Post 1 of 9

Delegating decisions: why AI needs real-time, trusted decisions to act

Decision engineering for the agentic enterprise

Enterprise AI is moving beyond answers and recommendations. AI systems are starting to take part in business processes, making decisions and initiating actions in the systems that move money and goods. That creates a new requirement: an enterprise must be able to show that each of these decisions used current information, followed business policy, was carried out safely and left reliable evidence of what happened. Agent frameworks, data platforms and governance tools each solve a piece of the puzzle, and none of them talk to each other out of the box. Getting an AI agent to make a good decision is hard; making sure hundreds of independent decisions lead to actions the business can control, trust and account for is harder. Wiring those tools into a single, working architecture is what I call decision engineering: building systems where AI-driven decisions are fast, bounded, measurable and provable.

00Summary

The argument in brief

  1. AI is moving from generating answers to making business decisions and initiating actions. The potential benefits are faster decisions, more work completed without manual intervention and a lower cost for each decision.
  2. You can only delegate a high-stakes decision when four things are true: it runs on live facts; it routes to the right decider; it passes hard software checks before acting; and it leaves behind enough evidence to prove it actually worked.
  3. In the airport thunderstorm example, rules take 80% of the decision evaluations and agents only 4.5%, yet agents carry about 40% of the money at stake, meaning the financial consequences tied to their decisions are the most significant. The numbers are illustrative, but the point holds: routing each decision to the right kind of decider is where the economic case starts. Measuring the cost, quality and outcome of each decision shows whether it's paying off.
  4. My reading of the last three years is that each wave of generative AI needed a new engineering practice before its results could be relied on. This wave needs one as well, and I call it decision engineering. It provides the mechanisms by which decisions are controlled, recorded and assessed, so that trust in them is justified.
  5. At the center is an append-only event log, the shared spinal cord of the architecture. Every rule, model, and agent reads its context from the log and writes its decisions and outcomes back to it. It is the foundation of the architecture, though not the whole of it. Trust also requires controls that are enforced before an action takes effect, and audit controls that preserve the evidence and protect it from alteration.
  6. Put simply: every consequential business decision should have one verifiable record that connects its context, its authority, its execution and its outcome, whether a person, a rule, a model or an AI agent made it. Everything else in this series exists to produce that record.
  7. The technology exists today, with Confluent, now part of IBM, providing the real-time foundation. What most enterprises lack is the engineering practice that connects it to their decisions, their agents and the systems that hold their business records.

01The problem

From answers to actions

Automated decisions are not new. Credit scoring, fraud detection and industrial control have relied on them for decades, each built for one narrow, well-structured decision. Generative AI makes decisions differently from the automation that came before it. It first came into wide use helping people find information, write content and complete tasks, with a person reading every answer. Now enterprises are moving it into the role the older systems held, across a far wider range of decisions, including ones that depend on unstructured information and span several systems. There, AI recommends and initiates actions that have financial, operational and customer consequences: rebooking a passenger, releasing a payment, reordering stock or dispatching a crew.

A chief information officer or chief AI officer therefore faces a practical question: how can the enterprise safely let AI make more consequential decisions, using the systems and data it already has? A hallucination in a chat window gets fixed with an eye-roll and a backspace. A hallucination in SAP or your reservation system moves real money or strands a real customer before anyone even knows it happened.

Two measures of delay describe the problem. Decision latency is the time between something happening in the business and someone or something acting on it. It is measured to the point at which the action is confirmed in the system of record, because a fast recommendation does not help if the action that follows it is slow. A complex insurance claim can take days or weeks, and a commercial credit decision can pass through several reviews, systems and teams. This delay often has no single owner and never appears as a line in a budget, yet its cost shows up everywhere: in lost sales, write-offs and the staff whose job is to move cases along a queue.

Governance latency is the time between a control being violated and the organization knowing about it. Closing that gap takes monitoring; explaining the violation afterwards takes records that show which data, permissions and policies applied to that single decision. Most enterprises already have those records, just scattered across different systems. AI that can't explain itself stays in pilot purgatory. Governance isn't a tax on speed; it's the entry ticket to production, which is why governance latency belongs alongside decision latency as a standard measure.

Delegating a high-stakes decision requires four capabilities:

CapabilityBusiness questionBusiness value
Real-time contextIs the decision based on information that is current and consistent?Fewer wrong decisions caused by stale or conflicting data
DecisionsIs each decision made by the right decider, whether a rule, a model, an agent or a person?Faster processing and a lower cost for each decision
Controlled actionsCan AI act only within defined business and security limits?Lower operational risk, and more work that can be automated safely
TrustCan the enterprise show who decided, why, under what authority and with what result?Accountability, compliance and the confidence to extend autonomy further

Posts 5 to 7 describe these capabilities in detail. The problem they address does not depend on having many agents; it arises whenever several independent actors, human or automated, can affect the same business process while working from overlapping information and holding different levels of authority. Even hundreds of agents rarely work together on one decision. What they do is make many different decisions that affect the same operation and compete for the same resources, as the next post will show.

Not every decision needs all four capabilities. A single low-risk decision inside a well-run application is fine with that application's own logic, transactions and audit log. These capabilities earn their keep when facts change across several systems, multiple actors are involved, processes run for hours or days, or a wrong action has real consequences.

02The arc

From prompts to decisions

The engineering focus of generative AI has moved quickly since 2023, and I break it into five stages. Each year the amount of work an AI system can do unsupervised has grown, and each increase has needed a new engineering discipline before the result could be relied on.

The five stagesA deeper definition of each stage
  • Prompt engineering is the craft of wording an instruction so that a language model gives a useful answer.
  • Retrieval engineering adds an organization's own documents and data to the prompt, a technique known as retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), so that the answer rests on facts the model was not trained on.
  • Context engineering manages everything the model sees at each step of a longer task, including its instructions, its memory of earlier steps and the results of the tools it has called.
  • Harness engineering builds the environment around the model: the tools it may use, the tests that check its work, the permissions that limit it and the feedback that lets it correct itself. An agent is commonly described as a model plus a harness. Anthropic now says the same about safety: a well-trained model can still be exploited through a poorly configured harness or an overly permissive tool.[49] Research systems can now refine a harness automatically, from its prompt and guardrails to its tools and skills, while the model stays the same.[18] The harness may be synthesized or evolved by the system itself; the controls around it may not, which is the boundary between harness engineering and what this series describes.
  • Decision engineering, the subject of this series, designs, routes, controls and records the decisions that AI makes or assists, so that each one can be trusted. A trusted decision is attributable, explainable, reconstructable, bounded, reversible where possible and evaluated for quality. Post 7 defines each of these terms.

In practice the stages overlap, but the terms are in wide use. OpenAI gave harness engineering currency in February 2026 with an account of how its own teams build the scaffolding and controls around coding agents.[12] Prompt, context and harness engineering are now described as successive phases of one maturity curve.[13] The fifth stage doesn't have an industry buzzword yet, so I'm staking a claim on decision engineering. Gartner already defines decision intelligence as engineering how decisions are made.[41] I use decision engineering for the runtime layer beneath that, which nobody has yet pulled together end to end: the records, the controls, the latency and the cost per decision, and what happens when rules, predictive models, AI agents and humans have to make calls together in real time. I use the term process agent for an agent that takes part in a business process as a full actor, working with other agents and with people over hours or days.

Why coding agents came first

Coding agents became useful before other kinds of agents because software development already had a harness. They still produce faulty changes, but the means of catching and reversing those changes were already in place. A compiler rejects malformed code, a test suite catches many errors and gives evidence that the work is correct, and version control records every change and its author. A sandbox limits the damage a mistake can cause, and a single command can revert a bad code change. But the effects a change has already had in production, such as altered data or completed transactions, often cannot be reverted the same way. Harness engineering largely consisted of connecting agents to machinery that software teams had built over several decades. Anthropic's auto mode for its Claude Code agent makes the point directly: file edits inside a project run without a check because version control can undo them, while shell commands, web requests and calls to outside tools go to a separate classifier first.[48]

You can't run git revert on a wired payment, and there is no compiler for a rebooked family of four. Decision engineering is the work of building that missing safety net for the rest of the business:

What a coding agent already hasWhat it providesWhat must be built for a process agent
Version controlA record of every change, in order, with its authorAn event log that records every signal, decision and action, each tied to the identity of the agent responsible
Test suiteEvidence that work is correct before it is releasedThe ability to re-run recorded events through a decision and compare the results, both before a change goes live and after the fact
Compiler and type checkerRejection of malformed workSchemas, which define the structure every event must have, enforced at the point where events are written
SandboxA limit on what a mistake can affectLimits on what each agent may spend, change and call
Code reviewApproval by a second party before a change is mergedReview of an agent's plan before it takes a consequential action
RevertA way to undo a changeA classification of every action as reversible, compensable (offset by a later action, such as a refund) or irreversible, with tested compensating actions and stricter approval for actions that cannot be undone

A harness keeps one agent from hallucinating in the dark. Decision engineering keeps 470 of them from bankrupting you in broad daylight.

03The value

Measuring the value

Automating a bad decision just lets you lose money at machine speed. If your automation rate goes up alongside refunds, rework, and compliance fines, you haven't improved the business, which is why these metrics have to be read as a set.

What is measuredMeasure
Faster responseDecision latency: the time from a business event to a confirmed action, with detection, decision, authorization and execution timed separately
Faster detection of control failuresGovernance latency: the time from a control being violated to the organization knowing
More automationShare of decisions resolved without manual intervention
Lower costCost of each completed decision or case
Better outcomes for customersA measure specific to the process, such as the share of passengers rebooked successfully or missed connections avoided
Lower riskNumber of duplicate, unauthorized or non-compliant actions prevented before they took effect
Better auditabilityShare of decisions with complete evidence that can be verified independently, and the time needed to investigate one decision
Total costThe full cost of the solution, including streaming, models, agents, integration, governance and human oversight

This series does not offer return-on-investment figures, because they depend on the process and the starting point. An enterprise should record a baseline for each measure on the decision it selects, before any change is made, and compare the same measures afterwards. The ninety-day plan in post 9 builds this in.
